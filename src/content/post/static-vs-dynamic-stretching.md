Dynamic stretching belongs in your warm-up, and static stretching is the better tool when you want your range to change over weeks. That's the short answer to static vs dynamic stretching, and it's roughly right; what it leaves out is that static stretching before training is less harmful than its reputation, dynamic stretching does less for lasting range than people assume, and neither one gives you range you can use under load.

Spend a few minutes in any gym before a run club or an early lifting session and you'll see both camps. Some people swing their legs and walk through lunges, others sit in a long hamstring stretch, and each side tends to think the other is doing it wrong. The argument usually goes nowhere because both sides are answering different questions. Once you name the goal, the comparison mostly sorts itself out.

## What Is the Difference Between Static and Dynamic Stretching?

Static stretching means holding a muscle in a lengthened position for a set amount of time, usually somewhere between a few seconds and a couple of minutes. Dynamic stretching means moving a joint repeatedly through its range at a controlled speed, like leg swings or walking lunges (1).

Two related terms get mixed into the same conversation. Ballistic stretching is a faster form of dynamic stretching that bounces into the end of the range (1). PNF stretching adds a muscle contraction to a stretch: you contract the stretched muscle, relax, and move a little deeper (1). For long-term range, PNF behaves a lot like static stretching, so most of what follows about static work applies to it too.

## What Static Stretching Does

In the short term, static stretching increases range, and the effect fades quickly; most of the acute gain is gone within about half an hour (2). That makes it a weak warm-up tool on its own and a reasonable one if you need a specific position for the session in front of you.

The bigger worry has always been strength and power. Here the dose decides almost everything. A systematic review of 106 studies found that static stretches shorter than 30 seconds had no meaningful effect on maximal performance, holds of 30 to 45 seconds had no significant effect either, and reductions became likely once a single stretch reached 60 seconds or more (3). A later review landed in the same place, with an average performance loss of about 4.6 percent after 60 seconds or more of stretching per muscle group and about 1.1 percent below 60 seconds (2). Those numbers come mostly from studies that tested people three to five minutes after stretching with nothing in between. When studies added dynamic activity after the stretching, the way a real warm-up does, the effect largely disappeared (2).

A 2018 trial makes that point directly. Twenty team-sport athletes did a full warm-up that included either 5 seconds of static stretching per muscle group, 30 seconds of static stretching, a set of dynamic stretches, or no stretching at all, and then practiced the tests before doing them. Sprint, jump, and change-of-direction results were the same in every condition (4). The longer case for what should fill a warm-up instead, including rehearsal sets, is in [active mobility, flexibility, and rehearsal sets](/active-mobility-flexibility-rehearsal-sets).

Over weeks, static stretching is one of the better-supported ways to gain range. The largest meta-analysis on long-term stretching (77 studies, 3,870 participants) found that static and PNF stretching produced larger range gains than ballistic or dynamic stretching (1). How much volume that takes, and why most people never get there, is covered in [how much stretching it takes to change your range](/how-much-stretching-does-it-take-to-change-your-range).

## What Dynamic Stretching Does

Dynamic stretching raises tissue temperature, takes joints through the ranges the session is about to use, and rehearses the movement. A review of the dynamic stretching literature found a substantial amount of evidence for short-term improvements in range and in the force, power, sprint, and jump performance that follow (5). The authors attributed the range change mostly to reduced stiffness of the muscle-tendon unit and the performance change to warmer tissue and the voluntary contractions involved (5). Faster ballistic versions looked less useful than controlled ones (5).

The performance effect is real but small. Across studies, dynamic stretching improved performance by about 1.3 percent when it was done within a few minutes of the activity (2).

Where dynamic stretching falls short is lasting range. In the long-term meta-analysis, ballistic and dynamic stretching produced roughly half the effect on range that static stretching did, and that conclusion rests on far fewer measurements (8 for dynamic and ballistic compared with 66 for static) (1). The authors suggested time under tension as one explanation; a stretch you hold keeps the tissue at length the whole time, while a stretch you swing through only visits it (1).

## Dynamic vs. Static Stretching: Is Dynamic Better?

Better for what is the only honest way to answer. Set the three goals side by side and the comparison stops being a contest.

| Goal | Better tool | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Getting ready for today's session | Dynamic stretching and practice of the movement itself | Small performance benefit, warmer tissue, and rehearsal of the positions you're about to load |
| Changing your range over weeks | Static (or PNF) stretching | Larger long-term range gains in the research, as long as the weekly volume is there |
| Being able to use that range | Neither on its own | Range you can't produce force in stays a position you visit; loading and end-range isometrics fill that gap |

The third row is the one most comparisons skip, and it's where most people's frustration with stretching actually comes from.

## Static and Dynamic Stretching Exercises

Here are three paired examples for areas people stretch most often. Use the dynamic version before training and the static version after training or on its own, when range is the goal.

For the hamstrings, the static version is a supine strap stretch: lie on your back, loop a strap around one foot, straighten the knee, and raise the leg to a firm stretch with the opposite thigh and the back of the pelvis heavy on the floor. Hold 30 to 60 seconds for two or three rounds per side. The dynamic version is a front-to-back leg swing while holding a wall for balance, 10 to 12 controlled swings per side, starting small and letting the height build without rounding the low back to get it.

For the hip flexors, the static version is a half-kneeling stretch: back knee down on a pad, tuck the pelvis slightly so the low back doesn't arch, and shift forward until you feel the front of the back hip. Hold 30 to 60 seconds for two or three rounds per side. The dynamic version is a slow reverse lunge with an overhead reach on the side of the back leg, 6 to 8 reps per side, pausing for a second at the bottom.

For the calves, the static version is a wall calf stretch with the back knee straight, held 30 to 60 seconds, then repeated with the knee bent to bias the deeper calf muscle. The dynamic version is a knee-to-wall rock: front foot a few inches from the wall, heel down, and drive the knee forward toward the wall and back for 10 to 15 reps per side.

Before training, keep any single static hold under 60 seconds and follow it with dynamic work, based on the dose findings above (2, 3). After training, the timing matters less than doing it consistently.

## Where the Evidence Gets Messy

A few things limit how far any of this can be pushed. Most dynamic and static stretching studies use young, healthy, recreationally active people over a few weeks, and many of the long-term studies used fairly low total stretching volumes (1). The acute performance studies usually tested people minutes after stretching with nothing in between, which doesn't look much like the way anyone actually trains (2). The long-term meta-analysis also found signs of publication bias, meaning positive results were more likely to be published (1).

Injury prevention is the claim that most outruns the data. The systematic review of acute stretching found no clear effect of static or PNF stretching on all-cause or overuse injuries, and no injury data at all for dynamic stretching (2). It did suggest stretching within a warm-up may help with muscle injuries in particular, but that's a narrower claim than "stretching prevents injuries," and it's the most anyone can say right now.

## What Neither One Does

Range you can't control is range you can't use. A meta-analysis of 55 studies found that resistance training improved range of motion about as well as stretching did, with no significant difference between the two (6), which tells you that tissue responds to load at length at least as well as it responds to holding still at length. That's the argument in [flexibility is range, mobility is what you can do with it](/flexibility-is-range-mobility-is-what-you-can-do-with-it), and it's also why range found in a stretch so often disappears by the next session, which is the subject of [why mobility doesn't stick](/why-mobility-doesnt-stick). Loading the end of the range, with end-range isometrics or with strength work taken deep enough to count, is the piece that both static and dynamic stretching leave out. Motive has a good comparison of [static stretching and loaded stretching](https://www.movewithpurpose.com/static-stretching-vs-loaded-stretching) if you want the practical side, and if you'd rather follow that kind of training than build it yourself, [KINSTRETCH Online](https://www.movewithpurpose.com/kinstretch-online) is the class library my coaches and I teach from.

One safety note applies to both kinds of stretching. Stop if you feel sharp or pinching pain, numbness, or tingling, and be careful with either type if your joints are already very loose; the signs that stretching is working against you are covered in [when stretching makes pain worse](/when-stretching-makes-pain-worse-how-to-spot-the-red-flags).

## Putting It Together

Before training, do a general warm-up, then dynamic stretching and lighter sets of the movement you're about to train. A short static hold is fine if a position feels unavailable, as long as it stays under a minute and you move afterward. After training, or on a separate day, use static stretching if more range is what you need, and give it enough weekly volume to matter. And on its own schedule, train the range you have: load it, hold positions near the end of it with intent, and keep asking it to do work, because that's what decides if the range is still there next month.
