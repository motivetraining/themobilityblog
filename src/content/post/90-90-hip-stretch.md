Watch someone try the 90/90 hip stretch after seeing it online and you'll usually see the same thing. They sit down with one leg bent in front and one bent out to the side, lean back onto both hands, let the back hip float off the floor, and wait for a stretch to show up. A minute later they switch sides and call it done. The shape is right on paper, but the position is doing very little for them, and often it's the wrong version of the position for their hips in the first place.

The 90/90 is a seated hip rotation position: both knees bent to roughly 90 degrees, with the front hip in external rotation and the back hip in internal rotation. It's a tool, and a good one, but how you set it up, where you put your weight, and what you do once you're there should depend on what your hips actually need. That's the thread running through everything below.

## How to Set Up the 90/90 Hip Stretch

1. Sit on the floor and bring your front leg across your body, knee bent to about 90 degrees, with the outside of that shin and knee resting on the floor.
2. Take the back leg out to the side, knee also bent to about 90 degrees, so the inside of the knee and ankle face the floor and the shin points behind you.
3. Sit tall, with your ribs stacked over your pelvis.
4. Use your hands on the floor or on blocks beside you if you need them.

Treat those angles as a starting point. If you can't sit comfortably, change the position until you can reach what you're going for: sit on a block, close the angles, or shift your weight toward the hip you're trying to work. Sometimes that means bringing the feet in until they come close to touching the other leg's thigh, so the position is closer to a 45/45 than a 90/90. A 90/90 that looks textbook but has you fighting the whole time isn't training much of anything.

On the knees: your shins don't have to lie flat on the floor. Forcing a shin down is one of the fastest ways to twist the knee to make up for rotation the hip doesn't have yet. Let the foot or knee lift a little and let the hip earn its way down over time.

## What You Should Feel

The back hip, the one in internal rotation, should feel like an achy, capsular stretch on the outside of the hip. Some people also feel the adductors (the inner thigh) stretch on that side. What it shouldn't feel is sharp or pinchy. That kind of sensation is your cue to change the setup, not to push harder.

The front hip, the one in external rotation, will usually feel like a deep stretch in the glute and the rotators underneath it.

You can change where you feel it by moving your pelvis and shifting your weight. Lean or shift toward the front hip and you'll usually feel more of the front-hip stretch; shift toward the back hip and it takes more of the load. Small changes in how your pelvis sits can move the tension around noticeably, and exploring those different lines of tension is part of the work, not a distraction from it.

You'll also see this position called a 90/90 hip flexor stretch or a 90/90 hip opener. The hip flexor label doesn't fit well; neither hip goes into extension, which is what a hip flexor stretch needs. The 90/90 is about rotation.

## What the 90/90 Is Actually For

It's tempting to think of the 90/90 as a way to work both hips at once, since each leg is in a different rotation. In practice, you'll usually be focusing on one hip at a time. What the position gives you is an easy way to switch between the two, so you can work rotation on both sides, in both directions, without getting up off the floor.

It also tends to be revealing. I wouldn't use the 90/90 as a test to decide if someone is "tight," but if one hip is limited, sitting in it will usually make that obvious. Most often, the limited side is the back hip, because internal rotation is the range people lose first. I go into why that happens, and when limited rotation might be structural rather than trainable, in [Hip Internal Rotation: The Range Most People Lose](/hip-internal-rotation-the-range-most-people-lose). If you want a cleaner read on where your controlled rotation ends, a [hip CAR](/what-cars-are-actually-for) is a better assessment than the 90/90 itself.

It's also worth knowing how much hips vary between people. In one study of 105 healthy men aged 30 to 40, passive internal rotation ranged from 20 to 50 degrees and external rotation from 10 to 55, and the authors cautioned that individual variation was large enough that normal values should be used with care [(1)](https://actaorthop.org/actao/article/download/28654/33535/82106). Two people can do everything right and still look completely different in this position. Compare your own sides, and your own progress over time, rather than chasing a photo.

## The Faults That Waste It

### Leaning Back and Hanging on the Hands

Using your hands for support is fine. Leaning back and hanging your weight on them takes the load off the hips and tilts the pelvis away from the position you're trying to train. Bring your hands closer to your hips, or put blocks under them, so the support is light and you can stay upright.

### Letting the Back Hip Float Without Noticing

When the back hip runs out of internal rotation, it lifts off the floor and your weight drifts onto the front hip. There's nothing wrong with that by itself; shifting toward the front hip can be a deliberate choice when that's the hip you're working. The problem is when it happens by default and you think you're working the back hip when you aren't. If the back hip is the focus, raise the floor with a block so you can keep weight on it.

### Forcing the Knee

Pushing a knee or shin down to make the position look cleaner can load the knee in a twisting direction it doesn't love. The rotation needs to come from the hip. If a knee complains, sit higher, open the angles a little, or shift the focus to the other hip until the pressure lives where it should.

### Rounding to Go Deeper

When people lean forward to get more stretch, they often round through the low back instead of moving from the hips, which moves the stretch into the spine. Lengthen first, then move. Even an inch of real movement at the hip does more than a big fold through the back.

## If You Can't Get Into It Yet

This is the 90/90 hip stretch for beginners, but it's also just the honest version for anyone whose hips aren't ready for the full position. There are two common situations where people can't get comfortable at all.

The first is a back hip with very limited internal rotation. These people can't settle into the back leg no matter what they try, and the whole position feels like a fight. Sitting on a block or a low box reduces how much rotation the hip needs, and closing the angles helps too.

The second is a front hip with limited external rotation. Here the front leg usually bows into something closer to a butterfly position, with the knee pointing up and the whole thing feeling awkward. Again, raising the hips and letting the front shin angle back toward you, rather than forcing it across, makes the position workable.

In either case, it's fine to bias the position toward the hip that can access it, or toward the part of the range you can reach comfortably. The goal is to be somewhere you can actually work. You can progress the setup as the hips change.

## Making It Active

Passive time in the 90/90 can make the position more comfortable, but range you can't control tends to disappear when you need it, which is the core idea behind [Flexibility Is Range. Mobility Is What You Can Do With It](/flexibility-is-range-mobility-is-what-you-can-do-with-it). The way to change that is to start loading the stretch while you're in it.

### Start With Rotational Isometrics

Before anyone does lift-offs, I want them to find rotational isometrics in the position. Sitting in your 90/90, take the back hip (in internal rotation) and create the intent to rotate it the other way, into external rotation, without letting anything move. Then do the same with the front hip: from external rotation, create the intent to turn it inward. Build the effort gradually and keep breathing.

What you're doing is contracting the tissues that are being stretched, at length. That's what turns a passive stretch into training. There's reasonable research support for the general principle: a systematic review of isometric training found that working at longer muscle lengths produced more muscle growth than equal work at shorter lengths, and transferred better to dynamic performance [(2)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sms.13375). That review looked at strength and muscle size rather than range of motion, and it included relatively few studies, so take it as support for the idea of loading at length rather than proof of any specific drill.

As a starting point, a few gradual contractions of ten to twenty seconds per direction is plenty. Adjust from there based on how the hip responds.

If you want a full protocol for building new range this way, PAILs and RAILs are the structured version of this idea, and Motive's [PAILs and RAILs guide](https://www.movewithpurpose.com/pails-rails) walks through how a set works.

### Then Lift-Offs

Once you can create tension in the position, lift-offs ask you to actively move into the end of the range you're working. For the back hip, that means keeping the knee down and lifting the foot, which drives the hip further into internal rotation with your own muscles. Keep the knee on the floor. If the whole leg comes up, the hip starts flexing and abducting, which isn't wrong, but it takes the focus off rotation, and rotation is the point here. The movement can be tiny. A few millimeters of real, controlled lift counts for more than a big movement made by leaning or twisting.

Short holds of a few seconds and a handful of reps per side are a reasonable place to start. Some people cramp at first, which usually settles with practice.

### Transitions

Switching from one 90/90 to the other is where the position starts to look like movement. There's no single right way to do it. You can transition with the feet planted, with more or less help from your hands, slower or faster, depending on what you can do and control without pain. What matters is the focus: you're training the rotational capacity of the hips through the transition, so think about pulling or pushing yourself into each position rather than throwing the legs around and landing wherever they end up.

### Where It Goes From Here

There's a lot you can build from the 90/90: adding load, moving into lunges or standing from the position, or using it as a base for other end-range work. Which of those makes sense depends on the person, which is the real answer to most questions about this position.

## How Often, and Where It Fits

There isn't a single right answer here, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who gives you one without knowing your hips. Someone chasing internal rotation in one stubborn hip needs something different from someone using the 90/90 to stay mobile alongside their lifting. In general, frequent, shorter exposures tend to work better than occasional long sessions, which is a big part of [Why Mobility Doesn't Stick](/why-mobility-doesnt-stick). If you're trying to fit it into a broader program, the coaches in [How Do You Structure Mobility Into Your Workout Routine?](/how-do-you-structure-mobility-into-your-workout-routine) share several approaches.

## When to Stop

Knee pain is the most common reason to back off. It usually comes from forcing a shin flat or twisting at the knee instead of rotating at the hip. Raise the hips, open the angles, or shift the focus to the other side.

A sharp, pinching feeling at the front of the hip, as opposed to the achy stretch on the outside, is worth paying attention to. Sometimes small setup changes clear it up. If they don't, it can point to the kind of bony impingement covered in the [hip internal rotation](/hip-internal-rotation-the-range-most-people-lose) post, and that's a reason to get the hip assessed rather than push through.

Stop and see a clinician for sharp pain, numbness or tingling down the leg, a hard bony stop that never changes, or hip pain that shows up at night. Some hips are built with less rotation and will never sit in a textbook 90/90, and that's fine. The point is to own the range your hips have and build what's available to you.

## A Tool, Used for a Reason

The 90/90 is useful because it's adaptable. You can bias it toward one hip or the other, raise it, narrow it, load it, and move through it, and each of those choices changes what it trains. The mistake is treating it as a fixed exercise everyone should do the same way. Start from where your hips are, pay attention to what you feel, and build from there.
