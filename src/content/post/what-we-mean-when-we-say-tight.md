*This one is written for coaches and trainers. If you're reading it as someone who trains, the short version is that "tight" describes how something feels rather than what's causing it, and a good coach will want to find out what's behind it before handing you a stretch.*

Tight might be the most common word in mobility, and it's one of the least precise. People say their hamstrings are tight, their hips are tight, their upper back is tight, and coaches say it right back to them. It sounds like a description. It works more like a diagnosis, because the moment you accept "tight" as the problem, you've also accepted the fix, which is to stretch the thing until it isn't.

A lot of the vocabulary in this field works that way. Tight, passive, activation, weak; each of these words carries a small theory about what's going on in the body, and the theory comes along every time the word gets used, even when nobody stops to check it. Most of the time the word is close enough to be useful. The trouble is that close enough can send a coach and a client in the wrong direction for months.

## Tight Is a Report

When someone tells me something feels tight, I take that as true. What I don't take as true is the implied mechanism, which is that the tissue is short and needs to be longer. Tight is a report of a sensation, and that sensation can come from a lot of places: tissue that really is stiff, a nervous system guarding a range it doesn't trust, a joint with passive range but no control at the end of it, a sensitive nerve, or plain fatigue.

The research on how loosely the feeling tracks the mechanics is worth knowing. In a set of experiments on people with chronic back pain, the ones who reported feeling stiff didn't have measurably stiffer spines than the ones who didn't. What they did have was a tendency to overestimate how much force was being pushed into their backs, which the authors interpreted as a protective response rather than a mechanical one [(1)](https://dx.doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-09429-1). That's a specific population with pain, so I wouldn't stretch it to every tight hamstring. It does make the point that the feeling and the tissue are two different measurements.

The stretching research adds to that picture. For years the standard line was that stretching gains were mostly about tolerance, the nervous system letting you go further before it complains. The most thorough analysis I've found, a 2025 meta-analysis of 65 studies, lands somewhere more interesting. Both a single session and longer programs of static stretching reduced overall stiffness; tolerance to stretch only increased with longer programs; and neither one changed fascicle length, which is the closest thing in those studies to the muscle actually getting longer [(2)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12152101/). So the tissue does change, and perception changes too, but "lengthening a short muscle" doesn't describe either one very well. The word tight quietly assumes the one mechanism the evidence supports least.

The clearest case for taking this seriously is the hypermobile client who feels tight everywhere. They often have more passive range than they could ever need, and stretching is the last thing that helps. What usually helps is strength and stability work that gives those joints some control, which is where organizations like the [Ehlers-Danlos Society](https://www.ehlers-danlos.com/physical-therapy/) put the emphasis too. I went into this in [When Stretching Makes Pain Worse](/when-stretching-makes-pain-worse-how-to-spot-the-red-flags). If "tight" always meant "short," that client wouldn't exist.

So the word starts the assessment rather than finishing it. A hamstring that feels tight in a toe touch might belong to a hip that doesn't flex well, might be reacting to a sciatic nerve that's sensitive to tension, or might have full range on a table and no ability to use it standing up. The nerve piece isn't hypothetical. A recent meta-analysis of 30 trials found that neurodynamic techniques (movements that slide or tension the nerve rather than stretch the muscle) improved straight leg raise range more than static stretching did, by about three and a half degrees on average [(3)](https://iris.unimore.it/handle/11380/1389791). That's a modest difference, and the authors rated the certainty of the evidence as low, but it's enough to say that some of what people call hamstring tightness lives somewhere other than the hamstring.

## Passive in Quotes

"Passive stretching" is a smaller problem, but it's one I run into constantly in my own corner of the field. In general usage it means sinking into a position and letting gravity or a strap do the work. In FRC, the long holds we prescribe get called passive too, and they aren't, really. You're being cued, you're breathing into the position, you're creating tension at points through the hold, and you're paying attention to what the joint is doing. That's why I say passive in quotes when I describe them.

The distinction matters because of what each version trains. Hanging out in a position mostly teaches the nervous system to tolerate it. Working in a position, with tension and intent, starts to give the body a reason to own the range. If a client hears "passive" and pictures a strap and a phone, they're going to do the first thing when you meant the second. There's a related split between passive and active range (how far a joint can go with help versus how far you can take it yourself), and the gap between those two numbers is one of the most useful things you can measure. I covered that in [Flexibility Is Range, Not Mobility](/flexibility-is-range-mobility-is-what-you-can-do-with-it).

## What Activation Is Covering For

"Glute activation" is the one I'd retire if I could. The phrase implies a switch; the glutes are off, a few band walks turn them on, and now you're ready to squat. I've never assessed a healthy person whose glutes were off. What I see constantly is a hip that hasn't learned to extend without the low back taking over, or a pelvis that isn't in a position where the glutes can do their job well.

To be fair to the idea, there's a real version of it. When a hip joint is irritated, the glutes can be inhibited. In one study, injecting fluid into the hip joint reduced gluteus maximus activity, and the lumbar extensors picked up the slack [(4)](https://www.backfitpro.com/medical-scientific-articles/2013/%5B51%5DFreeman,S.%282013%29Arthrogenic-neuromuscular-inhibition%5BClin.Biomech.%5D.pdf). That's a genuine mechanism, and it looks a lot like the "low back takes over" pattern coaches see. But it's an argument for figuring out why the hip is irritated, which is an assessment question. It isn't an argument for band walks before every session.

The warm-up research is mixed in a way that's worth being honest about. Some studies found small improvements in jump power or sprint times after glute-focused warm-ups, and others found nothing or found jump height got worse. A study on professional rugby players found no change in performance after an activation warm-up, though there was a trend toward the glutes producing more force, and the authors argued that some of the earlier positive findings had been overstated [(5)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530111/). So these drills may do something. They raise temperature, they give the person a chance to feel a position before loading it, and they may change how the hip organizes for a little while. The word just oversells the mechanism, and the oversell has a cost; people come to believe their glutes are broken in a way that needs fixing before every session, forever.

What's usually happening is a motor control problem and a positional problem, and it's worth calling it that. Motor control is how the nervous system organizes a movement: which joints move, in what order, and how much. Position is where the bones are when you ask the muscle to work. Both get better by teaching the movement, setting up the position, and loading it enough that the new organization sticks. I argued in [Mobility Isn't a Warm-Up. It's the Work.](/mobility-isnt-a-warm-up-its-the-work) that a few quick drills before lifting rarely change much on their own, and activation is a good example of why.

## Weak at What

"Weak glutes," "weak core," and "weak hips" have the same problem in a different shape. Weak relative to what, and at which joint angle? Strength is specific to position, especially at the ends of a range. Someone can be strong in the middle of hip extension and have almost nothing at end range, and someone else can be the reverse. The isometric research makes this point clearly; isometric training tends to produce strength that's specific to the joint angles you train at, which is part of why it's useful for building capacity exactly where it's missing [(6)](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-0863-4539). I wrote about that in [Isometrics: Infinite Tension, Infinite Potential](/isometrics-infinite-tension-infinite-potential).

So "weak" without a position attached is an incomplete sentence. The more useful version names where the strength runs out, which also tells you where to train.

## What the Words Do to the Client

Precision matters for coaches because it changes the intervention. For clients it matters for a second reason, which is that the words become part of how they understand their own body.

There's good evidence for this in back pain. In interviews with people who had acute and chronic low back pain, the things clinicians had told them shaped how they understood their symptoms and what they expected to happen, sometimes for years. Many of those messages were heard as "your back needs protecting." Reassurance from a clinician, on the other hand, made people more confident and changed how they approached movement [(7)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3823723/). That study was about clinicians and back pain, not coaches and tight hips, so I'm extending it carefully. But it matches what I've seen. Tell someone they're tight, and they'll often stretch for years without asking if it's working. Tell someone their glutes don't fire, and they'll believe a big part of them is offline. Tell someone they're out of alignment, and you've handed them a picture of a fragile structure.

The better option is to describe what's happening in plain words. "Your hip stops turning in here, and your low back picks up the rest." "You can get into this position, but you don't own the last part of it yet." "Your hamstring starts guarding when you get here." Each of those tells the client something true and points toward what to do, without calling any part of them broken.

## When Shorthand Is Fine

None of this means correcting every client who says "tight." That would be exhausting for everyone, and it misses the point; the client is telling you how something feels, and that information is valuable as it is. Shorthand is also fine between coaches who already share the underlying model, because the word is standing in for an idea both of them hold.

Precision has to live in the coach's own thinking. You can say "tight" in conversation all day as long as you aren't treating it as a finding. The question underneath every one of these words is the same: what is actually happening at the joint, and how do you know? A coach who can answer that can use whatever words the client understands. A coach who can't is letting the vocabulary do the assessment for them.
