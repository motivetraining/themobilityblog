One of the most common things I hear from people is that stretching doesn't work for them. They've been stretching their hamstrings or their hip flexors for months, the range hasn't budged, and they've come to the reasonable conclusion that their body just isn't built to change. When that comes up, I usually steer the conversation away from stretching for a minute and ask them about sitting instead. How many hours a day are they in a chair? How many years have they been doing that? And has their body adapted to it? The answer to the last question is almost always yes, and they can usually feel it the moment they stand up after a long drive. Their tissues have spent thousands of hours learning a position, and they're surprised that a couple of minutes of stretching a few times a week hasn't undone it.

That comparison isn't perfect (and I'll get to where it breaks down), but it reframes the question, because most stretching routines ask the body to change with a fraction of the time and attention it took to get there in the first place. So how much stretching does it take to change your range? The research has more to say on that than the "hold it for 30 seconds" advice most of us grew up with, and some of the studies people cite as proof that duration doesn't matter turn out to be studying something much narrower than you'd assume.

## Your Body Adapts to the Positions You Live In

The idea that tissue adapts to the lengths it spends time at has decent support, though most of the clearest evidence comes from extreme conditions. In classic animal studies from the 1970s, muscles held in a shortened position by a cast lost a large share of their sarcomeres in series and became considerably less extensible, while muscles held in a lengthened position added sarcomeres ([1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331536/)). The muscle was resetting its working length to match the position it lived in. A later study found that stretching immobilized muscle for as little as half an hour a day was enough to prevent that loss and even add sarcomeres ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1004076/)). And in 2021, researchers found substantially fewer sarcomeres in series in the chronically shortened biceps of people living with hemiparetic stroke, which was the first direct evidence of the same adaptation in human muscle ([3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8256086/)).

Sitting at a desk isn't a plaster cast, and I don't want to overstate the connection. Nobody has shown that a desk job removes sarcomeres from your hip flexors the way a cast does in a cat's soleus. But the principle those studies point to is the one I care about here: the body takes the positions you spend long periods in, and the way you load (or don't load) them, as information about how it should be built. If that's true for the positions you drift into all day without thinking, it should be true for the positions you hold on purpose. The difference is dose. Most people give the stretch a tiny fraction of the time they give the chair and then decide the stretch doesn't work.

## Three Different Things We Call Change

It helps to be clear about what "change" means, because when someone tells me their range improved from stretching, they could mean one of three things, and each one runs on its own timeline.

The first is tolerance. Your nervous system lets you go further into a position before it calls it. The tissue may not have changed much at all; what's changed is how much stretch you're willing (and allowed) to sit in. Tolerance can show up within a single session, and it fades about as quickly.

The second is a change in the tissue itself, where the muscle-tendon unit gets less stiff or the muscle adds length or size. This is what most people assume is happening when they stretch, and it's the slowest and hardest change to produce.

The third is usable range, meaning you can get into the position under your own control and produce force there. That's the one we care about on this site, because it's the difference between [flexibility and mobility](/flexibility-is-range-mobility-is-what-you-can-do-with-it), and a passive stretch on its own won't give it to you.

Keeping those apart makes the research much easier to read, since a lot of the arguments about stretching come down to people measuring one kind of change and talking about another.

## What the Stretching Research Actually Tested

The largest recent look at this is a 2024 meta-analysis that pooled 77 studies on stretch training. It found that stretching programs lasting two weeks or longer produced a moderate increase in range of motion compared to controls, and that static and PNF stretching outperformed ballistic and dynamic work ([4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10980866)). It also found that neither the total duration, the intensity, nor the weekly frequency of stretching significantly predicted how much range people gained, and that's the finding that tends to get passed around as proof that holding a stretch longer is a waste of time.

It's a useful paper, and the first part of that conclusion is worth taking at face value: stretching changes range. The second part deserves a closer look at what was actually tested. Of the 85 measures in the analysis that compared stretching techniques, 66 were static stretching. The authors deliberately excluded any study that combined stretching with another treatment, including strength training. And they note in their own discussion that the lack of a dose response is likely because most of the included studies used relatively low total stretch durations (around 1,000 seconds, or roughly 17 minutes), with only a few looking at anything more substantial ([4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10980866)). They also acknowledge that changes to the muscle-tendon unit itself have been suggested to require high-volume stretching, with lower volumes working mainly through tolerance.

So what does "duration doesn't matter" mean, given that design? As far as I can tell, it means that within a fairly narrow band of short, mostly passive, unloaded stretching, doing a bit more of the same didn't add much. That's a long way from showing that a long, intense hold followed by loading the tissue at that length is no better than a quick stretch. That approach was never in the data set. And if your stretching looks like the stretching in those studies (a few short holds, nothing after), it shouldn't be surprising that your results look like theirs too.

Other reviews fill in the picture. One found that time stretched within a single session didn't seem to affect range gains, but total time per week did, with roughly five minutes per week as a floor and more frequent stretching associated with better results ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/)). A 2018 meta-analysis of programs lasting three to eight weeks, averaging a little under 20 minutes of stretching per week, found trivial changes in muscle architecture and stiffness; what changed was the amount of stretch people could tolerate, and the authors concluded that adaptations to those shorter programs seem to happen mostly at a sensory level ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28801950/)). A larger 2025 analysis softened that somewhat, finding that chronic static stretching did reduce overall stiffness alongside increasing tolerance, though it didn't change fascicle length ([7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02204-7)).

Taken together, a typical stretching program produces real range, and a big share of that range is your nervous system getting more comfortable with the position. Tolerance is a legitimate adaptation, and it's often the first thing that has to change before anything else can, but it's more fragile than a tissue that has actually adapted.

## What Happens When the Dose Gets Big

If short programs mostly buy tolerance, what happens when the dose goes well past what anyone does in a normal session?

The most striking answer comes from a group in Germany led by Konstantin Warneke. In one study, participants used an orthosis to stretch the calf of one leg for an hour a day, every day, for six weeks. The stretched leg got stronger and gained range, and the gastrocnemius showed a significant increase in cross-sectional area ([8](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.878955)). The authors proposed that a stretch held that long creates enough mechanical tension to drive adaptations comparable to strength training.

I'd be careful with this one. It's a small body of work, mostly from one lab, mostly in the calves, using a device nobody is going to wear around the gym, and an hour a day is a proof of concept rather than a prescription. What it does show is that the tissue responds to stretching when the stimulus is large enough, which is exactly the part of the dose range the meta-analyses weren't built to see.

That lines up with how FRC approaches it, where a "passive" stretch (passive in quotes, because you're being cued and working inside the position the whole time) is held anywhere from two to fifteen minutes. From what I've seen, almost nobody does fifteen minutes, and plenty of people don't make it to two.

## Why the Range Doesn't Stay

Even when stretching works, the gains drift. A 2025 meta-analysis on detraining found that when people stopped a stretching program for two to six weeks, they lost some of their range, though they stayed moderately above where they started ([9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-025-00935-5)). That residual benefit is encouraging, and the loss matches something most people have felt: range that seems wide open after a good session and has quietly closed back down a week or two later.

My read (and this is where I'm leaning on coaching more than research) is that range you only ever visit passively is range your body has little reason to keep. I wrote about this in [why mobility doesn't stick](/why-mobility-doesnt-stick); if the only signal the tissue gets is that it can go somewhere when you relax into it, the signal is incomplete. Loading sends a different signal. A 2023 meta-analysis found that resistance training improved range of motion about as well as stretching did ([10](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01804-x)), which tells me load builds range in its own right. Combining the two, a long enough hold to get somewhere new and then strength at that length, is where I'd put my money, and it's also the combination the biggest stretching meta-analysis left out.

## How We Coach It

None of this produces a universal number, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who offers one. Where someone should be and why depends on their history, their joint structure, and what they need the range for. But this is how we approach it.

The cue we use for how long to hold a stretch is a physical sensation of release. At some point in a good hold, the position stops fighting you and the tissue lets go a little, and that's the moment we're waiting for. I can't tell you exactly how much of that release is the nervous system backing off and how much is the tissue itself responding to sustained tension (probably both), but it's a reliable signal that you've been in the position long enough to matter. It also takes longer than most people expect, especially in the joints that feel tightest, which is why we treat two minutes as the minimum rather than the target.

Past that, intensity and time go up together. Something around a seven out of ten intensity for somewhere near ten minutes is a dose I'd expect to produce change, but that's coaching judgment, and you won't find it in a study. It's subjective by nature, it shifts from person to person and joint to joint, and there's no right answer to chase. What matters more is that the dose is honest. If you're going to spend two minutes in a position, doing it most days is better than saving it up for one long session on the weekend, which also lines up with the research on weekly time ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/)).

How you spend that time matters as much as how much of it there is. The version I see most often is sinking into a stretch and waiting it out. Working inside it (breathing into the position, contracting into the stretch, then contracting out of it) is closer to how FRC structures PAILs and RAILs, and for the protocol itself I'll defer to [FRC's own material](https://functionalanatomyseminars.com/frs-system/functional-range-conditioning/).

Then the joint has to use what it just got. For a hip flexor stretch, that means getting the hip into extension and asking the glutes and hamstrings to hold it there without the low back borrowing the motion, using lift-offs, slow eccentrics, and loaded positions near the edge of what you own. For the hip rotators, the [90/90 post](/90-90-hip-stretch) walks through lift-offs in more detail. CARs fit in this phase too, though as I've written before, [CARs maintain and assess range](/what-cars-are-actually-for) more than they create it.

And the time frame has to be honest. If most of what you get in the first eight weeks is tolerance, judging the work at week three will mislead you, and so will judging it by how far you can sink on a good day.

## Who Needs a Different Approach

Everything above assumes the problem is range someone doesn't have. Plenty of people have the opposite problem. For hypermobile bodies, chasing more passive range can feed a cycle of instability and pain, and the work should shift toward control and strength in the range they already have. We covered the warning signs in [when stretching makes pain worse](/when-stretching-makes-pain-worse-how-to-spot-the-red-flags).

If a position produces sharp, pinching, or nerve-like symptoms, more time in it won't help, and that's worth taking to a clinician. Some restrictions aren't soft tissue at all, either. A bony block (we went through this with [ankle dorsiflexion](/ankle-dorsiflexion-what-restricts-it-how-to-test-it-and-rebuild-it)) won't respond to any amount of stretching, and forcing it can make things worse.

Longer holds also cost time, and time is the thing most people feel shortest on. Ten minutes per position adds up quickly, so most people are better served by picking the one or two restrictions that actually limit them and giving those real attention than by stretching everything a little.

## Back to the Chair

When someone tells me stretching doesn't work, I'm not dismissing what they've experienced. Their range really hasn't changed. But when we look at what they've been doing, it's usually a few short holds, a few times a week, with nothing asked of the tissue afterward, set against years of sitting in the opposite position for hours a day. The body has been responding the whole time; it's just been responding to the chair. If you want it to respond to the stretch, you have to give the stretch something closer to the time, patience, and effort the chair has been getting, and stop treating it as a box to check before the real workout starts.
