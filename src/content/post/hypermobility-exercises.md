The best hypermobility exercises build strength and control inside the range you already have. If your joints move further than most people's, you rarely need more range; what's usually missing is the ability to produce force and hold position near the ends of the range you've got, and that gets built slowly, with load, the same way any other strength gets built.

A lot of people who go looking for this have a similar history. They've always been the flexible one, stretching felt good so they did a lot of it, and somewhere along the way their hips, low back, or shoulders started aching after the classes that used to help. Most want to know what to do instead and if training is even safe for them. It is. This post is about training, though, and it can't diagnose anything; if any of the signs in the clinician section near the end sound familiar, start there.

## What Hypermobility Is

Joint hypermobility means a joint moves beyond the typical range for that joint. "Double-jointed" is the everyday term for the same thing (nobody has extra joints, just more motion in the ones they have), so if you came here looking for double-jointed exercises, you're in the right place.

The most common screen is the Beighton score, a nine-point check: one point for each little finger that bends back past 90 degrees, each thumb that folds down to touch the forearm, each elbow and each knee that extends more than 10 degrees past straight, and one point for placing your palms flat on the floor with your knees straight (1). The Ehlers-Danlos Society describes a positive score as 5 or more out of 9 for adults and 6 or more for children (1). It's a reliable screen when examiners use it (2), but it only describes range at a few joints and says nothing about why you hurt.

Having hypermobile joints and having a hypermobility disorder are different things. Plenty of people score high and never have a problem. When hypermobility comes with pain or other symptoms and doesn't fit a more specific syndrome, the 2017 classification calls it a hypermobility spectrum disorder (3). Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is a separate diagnosis with its own clinical criteria, and it's the one form of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome that still has no genetic test (4). A clinician can tell you which of those applies; a coach can't.

## Why More Stretching Is the Wrong Default

Stretching adds range, and range is what a hypermobile body already has in surplus. The relief after stretching tends to be short, and stretching more to chase it can keep pain going. That argument is made in full, along with what hypermobility looks like day to day, in [when stretching makes pain worse](/when-stretching-makes-pain-worse-how-to-spot-the-red-flags); this post picks up where it leaves off.

## What to Build Instead

The order I use is strength through the middle of the range, then control near the end of it, then load. Mobility is range you can control ([flexibility is range, mobility is what you can do with it](/flexibility-is-range-mobility-is-what-you-can-do-with-it)), and a hypermobile joint is often the clearest example of the gap.

How I work the end of the range depends on the assessment. If there's a big gap between how far a joint goes when someone moves it for you (passive range) and how far you can take it yourself (active range), I still use PAILs and RAILs, the end-range isometric method from Functional Range Conditioning, set inside the range the person already reaches and aimed at strength there, never at more range. If the gap is smaller, the work is positional isometrics: holding the joint actively in positions it can reach but can't yet control, so the nervous system learns to own them. Both are coached work, and PAILs and RAILs in particular are worth learning from someone who can watch you. (For how this plays out with clients in the studio, Motive has a page on [hypermobility and mobility training](https://www.movewithpurpose.com/hypermobility-and-mobility-training).)

Slow tempos and isometric holds suit this work because they keep you under tension in a position without bouncing into the end of the joint, and isometrics build strength in their own right; a systematic review found that holds at longer muscle lengths produced more growth and that high-intensity holds were needed for tendon adaptations (5).

CARs fit here as practice, not as a way to gain range. A slow, controlled joint circle only reaches as far as your control does, which makes it a good daily check on the range you own ([what CARs are actually for](/what-cars-are-actually-for)). If you'd rather follow guided control work than build it yourself, [KINSTRETCH Online](https://www.movewithpurpose.com/kinstretch-online) is the class library my coaches and I teach from.

## What Is the Best Exercise for Hypermobility?

There isn't one. The best exercise builds strength and control at the joint giving you trouble, in a range you can hold, progressed with load over months. A shoulder that subluxes needs different work from a knee that locks back, and the research hasn't found one type of exercise that beats the others (10).

## Hypermobility Exercises

The trials mostly test general strengthening programs, and only the shoulder research names specific exercises, so the choices below are coaching judgment built on that evidence. Progress each starting dose the way the strength training section describes.

### For Hypermobile Hips

Start with standing hip CARs. Hold a wall for balance, lift one knee to hip height, and draw the largest slow circle you can with the knee while the pelvis and trunk stay still. You should feel the work deep in the hip. The common fault is letting the pelvis tilt or rotate to make the circle bigger. Do two or three slow circles in each direction per side, daily.

Next is a seated 90/90 knee lift. Sit tall in a 90/90 position (front leg turned out, back leg turned in, both knees bent near 90 degrees), hands on the floor beside you if needed. Without leaning away, lift the back knee and foot an inch off the floor and hold. You should feel it on the outside of the back hip, and it may cramp at first. The common fault is leaning the trunk toward the front leg to fake the lift. Do three to five holds of 10 seconds per side.

### For Hypermobile Knees

Hypermobile knees often snap back past straight. Start with a wall sit at a knee angle you control, somewhere between 60 and 90 degrees of bend, with weight through the whole foot. You should feel the front of the thighs working hard. The common fault is drifting back onto the heels or letting the knees fall inward. Do three to five holds of 30 to 45 seconds.

Then progress to a single-leg stance with the knee a few degrees short of locked, held there without letting it creep back. You should feel the muscles around the knee working to keep the position. The common fault is drifting back into lockout as you fatigue. Do three holds of 30 seconds per side, then progress to slow step-downs from a low box, three seconds down, for three sets of 8 per side.

One trial in children with hypermobile knees compared exercising into the hypermobile range with exercising only to neutral, and knee pain improved by a similar amount in both groups (6). Avoiding the end of the range entirely isn't necessary; control there is the goal.

### For Shoulders

The best shoulder evidence comes from studies of heavy strengthening in people with hypermobility spectrum disorder. A 16-week rotator cuff and scapular program, three times a week, was safe and improved strength by roughly 28 to 31 percent (7). In the follow-up trial, heavy full-range training and light mid-range training produced similar pain trajectories, and the heavy loads caused little to no pain during exercise (8). Gains in external rotation and scaption strength tracked with better shoulder function (9).

For side-lying external rotation, lie on your side with a towel between your top elbow and ribs, elbow bent to 90 degrees, and rotate a light dumbbell up toward the ceiling and lower it over three seconds. You should feel the back of the shoulder. The common fault is letting the front of the shoulder roll forward at the top. Do three sets of 8 to 12 per side, with a weight that makes the last two reps hard.

For scaption raises, stand with light dumbbells, thumbs up, and raise your arms to shoulder height in a line about 30 degrees in front of your body. You should feel the top and back of the shoulders. The common faults are shrugging and arching the low back to finish the rep. Do three sets of 8 to 12.

### For the Trunk

Train the trunk to move and to hold. Segmental cat-camel on hands and knees is a good start: curl the spine into flexion one segment at a time from the tailbone up, then reverse into extension, as slowly as you can control. You should feel the movement spread across the whole spine. The common fault is hinging at the one or two segments that already move easily. Do three to five slow reps.

A side plank, from the knees or the feet, trains the trunk to hold. Stack the hips and shoulders, keep the bottom hip from sagging, and feel the side of the trunk working. The common fault is hanging on the bottom shoulder instead of pushing the floor away. Do three holds of 20 to 30 seconds per side, then progress to suitcase carries with one weight held at your side for three walks of about 30 meters per side.

## Strength Training for Hypermobility

Strength training for hypermobility follows the same principles as any strength training, with more attention to where each rep ends. Choose a range you can control on every rep, and add load before you add range. On each rep, stop short of the point where the joint "hangs" on its passive end (locked knees, elbows pushed past straight, shoulders sinking into their sockets). If you can hold the end position for a second without sinking, you own it.

Two or three sessions a week is reasonable; the shoulder trials used three a week for 16 weeks (7, 8). Progress in small jumps once a set feels controlled throughout, and treat these hypermobility strengthening exercises like strength work, with the last few reps of a set feeling hard.

Muscle soreness that fades within a day or two is normal. Joint pain past 48 hours, swelling, a new sense of the joint giving way, or spreading symptoms mean drop the load or range next session (that 48-hour line is my coaching rule, not a research finding). Isometric exercises for hypermobility are a good fallback on those days.

## Hypermobility Exercises to Avoid

Think of this as a list of things to change. Long passive holds at end range (the splits, deep yoga holds, sinking into a pigeon pose for minutes) can usually become active holds short of the end. Hanging on your joints at lockout (standing with locked knees, holding a plank or push-up with the elbows pushed past straight, dead-hanging from passive shoulders) can usually become the same position with a soft joint and muscles holding you there. And chasing depth for its own sake, in a squat or a stretch, can become depth you can control and reverse.

## Where the Evidence Is Thin

A 2014 systematic review found only four studies on exercise for joint hypermobility syndrome; people improved over time, but there was no convincing evidence that exercise beat a control group or that one type of exercise beat another (10). A 2022 review in Ehlers-Danlos syndrome found 10 studies with 330 participants, all showing some improvement, with only two rated high quality (11). The shoulder work is the strongest area and still involves fairly small groups (7, 8, 9).

The general direction (strength and control training helps, and heavier loads are more tolerable than people fear) has reasonable support. The specific exercises, the order, and how I use PAILs and RAILs and positional isometrics are coaching judgment on top of it.

## When to See a Clinician

A coach doesn't diagnose, and nothing here should stand in for an assessment. See a physician or a physical therapist who works with hypermobility if you have repeated dislocations or subluxations, pain at night or at rest, numbness or tingling, dizziness or fainting when you stand up, fatigue alongside widespread pain, or skin that bruises easily, stretches unusually far, scars poorly, or tears easily. Dislocations, fatigue, and dizziness on standing are all common features of hypermobility spectrum disorders (12), and skin fragility is one of the features that points toward Ehlers-Danlos syndromes (4). If you've already been diagnosed, bring your plan to that clinician before loading up.

## Start Where You Have Control

The work is slower and less dramatic than another stretching class, and it pays off in a way stretching never did. Pick one exercise for the area that bothers you most, start well inside the range you control, and add load before range. Own the middle of the joint first; the end tends to get easier once you do.
