## Search Intent

People who have been told they are hypermobile, or suspect it, and want to
know how to train safely. Many have been stretching for years because it
feels good and are now sore or unstable. Some have a diagnosis (hypermobility
spectrum disorder, hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome). They want exercises
and reassurance that training is allowed.

## Angle

A hypermobile body usually has plenty of range and not enough control of it.
More stretching adds to the side that is already in surplus. The work is
strength and control inside the range a person already has, built slowly,
with load, and that is ordinary strength training done with more attention
to the ends of the range.

This follows directly from the site's definition of mobility as range you
can control, so the post does not need to invent a position.

## Outline

1. **Intro (no heading).** Scene: the person who can drop into the splits,
   has always been "the flexible one", and whose hips and lower back ache
   after every class. Then the direct answer: what hypermobile people should
   train.
2. **## What Hypermobility Is.** One short section. Joints that move beyond
   the typical range; how it is screened (the Beighton score, described, not
   used to diagnose); the difference between being hypermobile and having a
   hypermobility disorder. Sourced, plain, and brief. Link the red-flags
   post for the fuller picture.
3. **## Why More Stretching Is the Wrong Default.** Two or three sentences
   and a link to the red-flags post. Do not re-argue it.
4. **## What to Build Instead.** Strength through the middle of the range
   first, then control near the end of it, then load. Why slow tempos and
   isometric holds are useful here.
5. **## Hypermobility Exercises.** A `###` per exercise, each with setup,
   the movement, what to feel, the most common fault (usually sinking into
   the end of the joint, such as locked knees or elbows), and a dose. Cover
   the areas people search for:
   - `### For Hypermobile Hips`
   - `### For Hypermobile Knees`
   - `### For Shoulders`
   - `### For the Trunk`
   Choose exercises that a clinical or research source supports for this
   population. Do not invent a protocol.
6. **## Strength Training for Hypermobility.** How to progress load, how to
   judge range on each rep (stop short of the passive end range, own the
   position), how often, and how to tell productive effort from a flare-up.
7. **## Hypermobility Exercises to Avoid.** Framed as what to change, not a
   banned list: long passive stretches into end range, hanging on joints at
   lockout, chasing depth for its own sake.
8. **## Where the Evidence Is Thin.** Trials are small and mixed. Say what
   the reviews conclude and what is coaching judgment.
9. **## When to See a Clinician.** Specific: repeated dislocations or
   subluxations, pain at night, numbness or tingling, dizziness or fainting,
   fatigue and widespread pain, easy bruising or skin that tears easily. A
   coach does not diagnose; say so once.
10. **Short practical wrap-up.**
11. **## References**

## Sources to Start From (verify each)

- The Beighton score: an original or validation source, plus the Ehlers-Danlos
  Society's description.
- Castori et al. 2017, American Journal of Medical Genetics Part C: a
  framework for the classification of joint hypermobility.
- Malfait et al. 2017, same journal: the 2017 international classification
  of the Ehlers-Danlos syndromes.
- Palmer et al.: systematic reviews of exercise for joint hypermobility
  syndrome (Physiotherapy, 2014, and any later update).
- Liaghat et al.: trials of heavy shoulder strengthening in people with
  hypermobility and shoulder symptoms.
- Any trial comparing exercising into the hypermobile range against
  exercising to neutral only, in children or adults.

If a source can't be opened and confirmed, drop the claim it was meant to
support.
